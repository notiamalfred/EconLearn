Competition and Allocation

Why allocation is needed

Scarcity forces a rule for deciding who gets what. The chain is:

  1. Scarcity
  2. Competing claims (competition)
  3. Allocation criterion
  4. Discrimination (who is selected)

Price competition

A type of competition based on users' willingness or ability to pay. The price need not be money. Exchanging a collectible toy for a ticket also reflects willingness and ability to pay.

Non-price competition

A type of competition based on criterion other than willingness or ability to pay, such as age, identity, queueing time, academic results, or a dress code.

Allocation mechanisms

  1. Tradition and custom
  2. Government decision
  3. Market exchange (also price competition)

Ask what criterion decides allocation. Money is not required for price competition, and paying money does not automatically make every selection rule price competition.

Goods vs Bads

Classifying

  1. Is it a good or a bad?
    • A good: some is preferred to none.
    • A bad: less is preferred to more.
  2. If it is a good, is it scarce?
    • Economic good: quantity is insufficient for all wants.
    • Free good: quantity is sufficient for all wants.

Supporting features

  • Cost involved in production? — Yes for economic goods, No for free goods.
  • More of them preferred? — Yes for economic goods, No for free goods.
  • Willing to pay a price for more? — Yes for economic goods, No for free goods.
  • Competition involved? — Yes for economic goods, No for free goods.

Free-of-charge ≠ free good. The definition turns on whether available quantity is sufficient to satisfy all wants, not whether the official money price is zero.

Economic Systems

Three questions that distinguish a system

  1. Who owns most resources? Government or private individuals and firms? Or does tradition decide?
  2. How are they mainly allocated? Tradition, government decisions, or market prices?

Traditional economy

Tackles the three basic economic problems mainly by customs and traditions.

Customs and traditions determine the ownership and allocation of most resources. People follow rules and ways of doing things passed down over time.

Example: In some New Territories villages, only males may share the property passed down by the family. Tradition decides who receives the property — this concerns for whom to produce.

Planned or command economy

Tackles the three basic economic problems mainly by government decisions.

Most resources are owned by the government and allocated by government decisions. The visible hand means the government directly decides how resources are allocated.

Example: In Hong Kong, an income limit helps decide who may apply for public housing. This non-price criterion uses household income, not the rent people offer to pay. The income rule concerns for whom to produce. But one government service does not make the whole economy a planned economy.

Market economy

Tackles the three basic economic problems mainly by the market mechanism.

Most resources are owned by private individuals (households and firms), and their allocation is mainly guided by market prices. The invisible hand refers to prices helping guide separate choices — no single authority has to decide every use of every resource.

Example (for whom to produce): A person willing and able to pay the fee can get tuition services from a private tutoring centre. The price decides who receives the service.

Example (how to produce): A firm compares the prices of AI chatbot services and labour when choosing its combination of factor inputs. The prices help it choose which resources to use.

Private Property Rights

Three exclusive rights

  • Exclusive right to use — only the owner can decide how to use the property; no one else can use it without the owner's permission.
  • Exclusive right to receive income — only the owner has the right to receive income generated from the property. Rent is payment for using property such as a house; licensing income is payment for permission to use property, such as a photograph. Ask who has the right to receive that income.
  • Right to transfer — passing the property rights to another person, for example by selling or giving away the good.

Clear delineation means it is clear who has each right and what that right allows. A prerequisite is something that must be in place first.

Why clear rights matter

  1. Private property rights are clear.
  2. The price mechanism can be used.
  3. Resources can go to highest-valued users.

Highest-valued users are those willing to pay the most for the resource. Clear rights let buyers and sellers know which rights are being exchanged.
