Scarcity & Cost

The foundation of every economic decision.

Scarcity Opportunity Cost Full Cost Three Traps

Scarcity

What scarcity means

Scarcity refers to the situation in which the resources available are not enough to satisfy all people's wants.

Scarcity concerns whether resources are sufficient relative to people's wants, not whether their physical amount is absolutely small. It is a comparison of quantity available versus size of want.

Examples

  • Not scarce: fresh air in an outdoor area
  • Scarce: fresh air in a fire scene

Scarcity does not imply unlimited wants. Even limited wants create scarcity when available resources are much smaller.

Opportunity Cost

The definition

The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the highest-valued option forgone.

Limited resources force a choice; some options are forgone; the value of the best option forgone is the opportunity cost.

A four-step method that works in most questions

  1. State the choice and the decision moment.
  2. Identify the scarce resources required — include money, time, effort, owned assets, and receipts forgone.
  3. Identify and state the value of the highest-valued option forgone.

Two values to distinguish

  • Benefit of the choice: the value of the option chosen to the decision maker.
  • Opportunity cost of the choice: the value of the highest-valued option forgone.

Preference order runs from the option with the highest value to the option with the lowest value.

Full Cost

Full cost is the opportunity cost

Full cost is the value (usually expressed in $) of the best alternative uses of all resources (money, time, satisfaction, etc.) necessarily forgone in making a choice.

Explicit cost

Money expenditure made as a result of the current choice — e.g. ticket payment.

Implicit cost

The value of the best alternative use of self-owned resources or receipts forgone — e.g. earnings, rent, resale value, or time.

Full cost = explicit cost + implicit cost

Check four kinds of resources before you cost a choice

  1. Money, time, and effort
  2. Income or receipts forgone
  3. Use of an asset already owned (e.g. a ticket, device, premises, another resource already held)

Three situations change opportunity cost

  1. The value of the current highest-valued option forgone rises or falls.
  2. The chosen option requires a different amount or type of scarce resource.
  3. A different option (a new option or an originally lower-ranked option) becomes the highest-valued option forgone.

Always identify what changed: the chosen option's benefit, the resources required, the identity of the best option forgone, or its value.

Three Opportunity-Cost Traps

1. Benefit trap

A rise in the value of the chosen option does not by itself change its opportunity cost.

2. Ranking trap

A changed preference order or a new option matters only if it changes the highest-valued option forgone.

3. Past-payment trap

Only options available at the decision moment can be forgone. A past cost that cannot now be recovered is excluded.

Remember

  • Higher opportunity cost need not reduce popularity.
  • Higher money cost need not mean higher full cost.
